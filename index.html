01 · Notice

Why do bees build hexagons?

Look inside any beehive and you will find thousands of perfectly identical six-sided cells, packed together with no gaps. Bees did not study geometry. Evolution found the answer for them.

What I noticed

Every cell has six equal walls and the cells share those walls. Honeycomb wax is expensive for bees to make (they eat roughly 8 kg of honey to produce 1 kg of wax), so using the least material for the most storage matters a lot.

The hexagon shows up everywhere

📷 Basalt columns

Basalt Columns

As lava cools it shrinks and cracks. Cracks spread to relieve stress most efficiently when they meet at about 120°, forming hexagonal columns like the Giant's Causeway.

📷 Dragonfly eye
images/dragonfly.jpg

Dragonfly Eyes

A dragonfly's compound eye holds up to about 30,000 tiny lenses, called ommatidia, packed in a hexagonal pattern so nothing is missed and no space is wasted.

📷 Plant cells
images/plant-cells.jpg

Plant Cells

Under a microscope many plant tissues show cells that average out to about six neighbors each. Growing cells push against each other and settle into near-hexagonal packing.

02 · Explain

The mathematics of efficiency

Only three regular polygons can tile a flat surface with no gaps: triangles, squares and hexagons. So which one is best?

Same area, shorter wall

Fix the area of each cell at 1 unit². The perimeter (wax needed) for each shape:

ShapePerimetervs. hexagon
Triangle4.56+20%
Square4.00+3.5%
Hexagon3.72best

The formulas

For a regular hexagon with side length s:

Area = (3√3 / 2) · s² ≈ 2.598 · s²
Perimeter = 6 · s

Each interior angle is 120°, and three cells meet at every corner (3 × 120° = 360°), so there are no gaps.

The Honeycomb Conjecture

The idea that the hexagonal grid divides a plane into equal-area regions with the least total perimeter was stated by Marcus Terentius Varro in antiquity and finally proved by mathematician Thomas Hales in 1999. Bees are nature's proof of concept.
